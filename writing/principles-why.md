# Writing principles, with the reason for each

For a writer, not a checker. Each principle names what the reader is doing at that moment and what the principle buys them. When a sentence breaks a principle on purpose, the reason beside it says what is being traded away. Sources: Gopen and Swan (reader expectations), Stanley's tips, Mensh and Kording, Zinsser, Hunt. Models: Murphy's papers, Stanley's 2011 novelty paper.

## The one idea behind all of them

A reader spends attention in two places: on working out *where* the information is in a sentence, and on the information itself. Every principle below moves attention from the first to the second. Structure is not decoration; it is the part of the sentence the reader interprets before the words.

## How the writer and the assistant share the page

- **The author drafts; the assistant edits as an advisor.** Why: the habit transfers only through the author's own attempts. An edit the author did not make teaches nothing and leaves a sentence in a voice the author will not recognize at the defense.
- **Keep the author's verbs, order, and interpretation; change only the error.** Why: a sentence carries a reading of the result. Changing the verb or the order changes the reading, and the assistant does not own the result.
- **Mark every big change (split, move, merge, add, reword a claim).** Why: the author decides what the text claims. A silent change is a claim the author never made.
- **Never add a claim or finding.** Why: an added sentence that reads well is the most dangerous kind; it passes review and says something the data were not asked.
- **Review the argument first, then paragraphs, then sentences, then words.** Why: a perfect sentence inside a wrong paragraph is wasted; a wrong claim placed well misleads faster.

## Order of work

- **Draft before rereading notes; pages every day; no rules while drafting** (Hunt). Why: prose is where the argument forms. Notes feel like progress and are not. Rules applied during drafting stop the thought before it lands; they belong to revision, which is the main work.
- **Then paragraphs, then sentences, then forbidden words.** Why: each pass changes what the next pass sees. Fix the claim and half the sentences rewrite themselves.

## Sentences

- **The subject is the method, the argument, or the quantity; never the authors.** Why: the reader takes the grammatical subject as whose story the sentence tells. "We" makes every sentence a story about the authors; the thing under study is pushed out of the topic position. (Gopen and Swan, topic position; Stanley.)
- **Subject meets verb at once.** Why: the reader holds the subject in memory until the verb arrives and reads everything in between as an interruption, therefore as unimportant. Material placed there is lost even when it mattered.
- **The verb names the action; never "use", no "is" where a verb exists, no nominalization where the verb would do.** Why: the reader looks to the verb for what happens. "Use" can stand for any action, so it tells the reader nothing and forces a guess; a nominalization ("the allocation of") hides the action in a noun and leaves the verb empty. (Gopen and Swan; Stanley's "use is the evil cousin of we".)
- **One point per sentence, the new point at the end.** Why: the reader emphasizes whatever a sentence ends on, the stress position. Two points compete and the reader cannot tell which the writer meant; a sentence that ends on a citation or a parenthesis stresses the wrong thing.
- **Old before new: open on a term the previous sentence left behind.** Why: the reader needs context before new information. A sentence that opens cold forces a backward search; a paragraph of cold openings reads as a list of facts, not an argument. The repeated term is the thread.
- **"This" carries a noun.** Why: a bare "this" points at anything in the previous sentence; the reader guesses, and in technical prose the guess is often wrong.
- **Length is governed by structure, not by count.** Why: the reader's cost is not word count but how long the subject waits for its verb and how many points compete. A 30-word sentence with one point and the verb early reads faster than two 12-word sentences that split a thought. A parallel sentence is long and cheap because the reader learns the pattern once. The 35-word flag is for review, not a limit: a sentence over it must pass the structural tests or be parallel. (Zinsser: clutter, not length; Mensh and Kording: parallel ideas take parallel wording.)
- **Vary rhythm: a long sentence, then a short one that carries the point.** Why: a run of equal sentences numbs the reader; the short one after a long one is heard.

## Paragraphs

- **Thesis sentence, setting, number against comparator, reclaim, link.** Why: a reader who stops after one sentence should have the point, and a reader who finishes should be able to say what the paragraph claimed in the evidence's own terms. The last sentence is the second stress position of the paragraph and the hand-off to the next. (Stanley's paragraphs; Mensh and Kording's context, content, conclusion.)
- **A chapter's first paragraph may open with setup and land the claim last.** Why: it has no previous paragraph to link to, and the setup is the old information the whole chapter stands on (Murphy's openers).
- **One paragraph, one claim.** Why: the reader files one claim per paragraph; a second one is lost or confused with the first.
- **Transitions name the logic.** Why: "moreover" tells the reader another sentence is coming; "yet" tells the reader how it relates. The reader needs the relation.

## Results

- **Question or prediction, then the number with its comparator, then the verdict, then the reading.** Why: the question tells the reader what the number will answer; the comparator in the same sentence is what gives the number meaning (0.33 means nothing until "against 0.11"); the verdict in its own sentence is the stress position the whole paragraph was built to reach; the reading last, so that the reader moves from evidence to meaning, not the reverse.
- **Open with the number when it is the surprise.** Why: the surprise *is* the thesis sentence; delaying it buries the point.
- **Never a bare number: comparator, sample, interval or test.** Why: a number without a comparator cannot be judged; without a sample it cannot be trusted; without an interval a gap cannot be told from noise. The reader will ask all three, so answer them where the number stands.
- **Tables carry comparisons; prose carries the point.** Why: a reader compares numbers down a column, not across sentences. Prose that lists six numbers makes the reader build the table in their head.
- **Caption: conclusion first, method second.** Why: a reader skims figures before text; the caption is often the only sentence read.
- **Label every number: result, control, threshold, selection.** Why: a selected cell reported as a result overstates the finding; the label keeps the reader's and the writer's honesty.

## Claims

- **Never hedge a number.** Why: the measured value is what the run gave; "roughly" or "seems to" adds doubt the data do not carry and signals the writer does not trust the measurement.
- **Bound the claim's reach once, beside it.** Why: the reader needs to know where the claim holds; the bound (corpus, seeds, model) is the honest form of a hedge, and once is enough because repetition reads as anxiety.
- **"May" and "can" only for mechanisms not yet tested, at most once per paragraph.** Why: a mechanism is a guess until measured and should sound like one; more than one guess per paragraph and the paragraph has no finding.
- **A claim's reach equals its evidence.** Why: "blind matching cannot read hidden states" invites the one counterexample; "four blind losses stayed at the control on one block of two models" cannot be refuted by it.
- **Close with what the result does not show.** Why: the reader who reaches the end is deciding how far to trust the section. Naming the limit there, once, earns the trust for everything before it.

## Words

- **No intensifiers ("very", "significantly" without a test, "remarkably", "boost").** Why: emphasis by adverb tells the reader the writer needed to advertise; emphasis by stress position lets the result speak. "The results were good" is stronger than "very good" (Stanley).
- **No filler ("in order to", "actually", "note that", "it is worth noting").** Why: words that add no information cost attention and signal nothing to the reader except hesitation.
- **One term per thing, from the glossary, defined once in apposition.** Why: a synonym makes the reader ask whether a new thing has entered; a coined name makes the reader learn a word that carries nothing. Define once, then the bare term.
- **Plain, exact words; standard terms stay standard.** Why: the simplest exact word is read fastest and cannot be mistaken for a claim of novelty.
- **"That" restricts, "which" adds; no em dashes; colon and semicolon make stress positions.** Why: the first keeps restrictive and parenthetical clauses apart for the reader; the dash is an all-purpose joint that hides which relation is meant; the colon and semicolon each give the sentence a second place to put a point.

## When to break a principle

Any of these may be broken on purpose, and the reader will read the break as deliberate only if the principle is kept everywhere else. Break "old before new" to startle with a result; break "subject meets verb" to hold a qualification the reader must see first; break "one point" for a parallel list. Say to yourself what the break trades away; if the answer is "nothing", it is not a deliberate break, it is a draft.
