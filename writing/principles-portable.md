# Writing principles (portable memory)

Scope: scientific prose for publication, dissertation chapters, and any assistant that edits them. Models: Kieran Murphy's papers and Kenneth Stanley's 2011 novelty-search paper. Sources: Gopen and Swan, "The Science of Scientific Writing"; Stanley's writing tips; Mensh and Kording, "Ten simple rules for structuring papers"; Zinsser, "On Writing Well"; Lynn Hunt, "How Writing Leads to Thinking".

## How an assistant works with me

- I draft; the assistant advises. The assistant points out each error with the rule in full and the reason, then corrects it with the smallest change that fixes it.
- Keep my language: my verbs, my sentence order, my interpretation. Change a verb only when it is "use", "utilize", a filler, or wrong.
- Mark every big change: a sentence split, moved, merged, added, or a claim reworded. Small changes (spelling, tense, agreement, a glossary term, a cut filler word) need no mark.
- Never add a claim, a finding, or an interpretation that my text did not contain. If a sentence seems missing, say so as a question; do not write it in.
- Order of a review: argument first (is the claim right and placed last?), then paragraph shape, then sentences, then words. Biggest point first.
- Numbers come from the reports; when one is wrong, correct it and name the source.

## Order of work (Hunt)

1. Draft to find the argument: pages every day, no rereading of notes to start, no rules while drafting.
2. Fix paragraphs. 3. Fix sentences. 4. Strip forbidden words. 5. Check against the models: would Murphy let the quantity act here; would Stanley state the thesis before the support.

## Sentences (Gopen and Swan, Stanley)

1. The subject is the method, the argument, or the quantity. Never "we", "our", "I".
2. Subject meets verb at once; nothing between them but one short modifier.
3. The verb names the action. Never "use", "utilize", "using"; no "is" where a verb exists; no nominalization where the verb would do.
4. One point per sentence; the new point at the end, in the stress position.
5. Old before new: each sentence opens on a term the previous sentence left the reader holding.
6. "This" always carries a noun.
7. Length is controlled by structure, not by count: a sentence that passes 2 and 4 with at most one subordinate clause is short enough. Parallel sentences may run long: items share grammar and order, at most four. Flag sentences over about 35 words for review; keep the ones that pass. Vary rhythm: a long sentence, then a short one that carries the point.

## Paragraphs (Stanley; Mensh and Kording)

- Shape: thesis sentence, setting, number against its comparator, reclaim of the thesis, link to the next paragraph.
- A chapter's first paragraph may open with setup and land the claim at its end.
- One paragraph, one claim.
- Transitions name the logic: "so", "yet", "by contrast", "the next question is". Not "additionally", "moreover", "furthermore".

## Results

- Default order: the question or the prediction; the number with its comparator in the same sentence; the verdict in its own short sentence; the reading last, linked forward.
- Choice point: open with the prediction when the result confirms it; open with the number when the result is the surprise or the chapter's main finding.
- Never a bare number: comparator (control, baseline, ceiling, carried setting), sample (seeds, prompts), interval or test when a gap is claimed.
- Tables carry comparisons across more than two cells; prose quotes the one or two numbers that carry the point.
- Figure captions: conclusion first, method second.
- Label every number: result, control, threshold, selection, sanity check.

## Claims and hedging

- Never hedge a number: no "about", "roughly", "seems to", "suggests that" on a measured value.
- Bound the claim's reach once, beside it: corpus, seeds, model, split. The bound is a fact, not a hedge word.
- "May" and "can" are for mechanisms not yet tested; at most one such sentence per paragraph.
- A claim's reach equals its evidence.
- Close a result with what it asserts but does not show, once, at the end of the section.

## Words

- Forbidden: "we", "our", "use" in any form, bare "this", "in order to", "actually", "essentially", "seems that", "very", "highly", "significantly" (unless a test is named), "remarkably", "dramatically", "interestingly", "note that", "it is worth noting", "boost", "leverage".
- Glossary terms: defined once at first use, in apposition, then repeated bare; no synonyms, no coined names.
- Plain vocabulary; the simplest exact word; standard terms stay standard.
- "That" restricts; "which" after a comma adds. No em dashes; a colon or semicolon opens a second stress position.

## Checklist before a section leaves my hands

- grep: "we", "our", "use", "using", "utilize", bare "this", "in order to", intensifiers: zero.
- First three words of each sentence name something already introduced (except paragraph openers).
- Each sentence: subject meets verb within four words; one point; the point at the end.
- Each sentence over 35 words passes the tests or is a parallel list of at most four items.
- Each paragraph: first sentence is the claim; last sentence restates it and links forward.
- Each number: comparator in the same sentence; seeds and prompt count; interval or test for a gap.
- Each result: question or prediction, number against comparator, verdict, reading.
- Each claim: reach bounded once; no hedge on a number; at most one "may" per paragraph.
- Each term: glossary wording at first use, bare afterward.
- The section ends with what it asserts but does not show.
