# Ken's Writing Tips (advisor's note)

Verbatim note supplied by the user 2026-09-11, full text supplied 2026-10-25. Scope, in Ken's words: the rules are for professional writing for publication; "in personal correspondence and other kinds of writing, these rules are unimportant." User ruling 2026-10-25: the "we", "this", and "use" rules apply to every output, chat included.

-Never say "we."  Remove all first person and second person from the text entirely.  Technical writing is not about the author(s) so the author should never be the subject of a sentence.  The subject is what you are talking about.  If this rule causes you to occasionally slip into passive voice, that's okay: passive voice is better than "we."  In fact, "we" is usually a crutch that lets you avoid having to think about the optimal sentence structure.  Look through my publications in recent years and you will see almost no "we" or "I" whatsoever.  The only exception is when I use "we" to refer to myself *and* the reader, as in "We must think about..." or "We can now..."  But such empathetic writing should be applied very sparingly and is not for novices.  It risks sounding condescending or pedantic.  I generally save it for climactic moments like the end of a discussion section where I am drawing lessons for "all of us."  Other than that, no "we."

-Never include the word "this" without an argument.  That is, never say, "This means..." or "This changed..."  You can write like that in literary writing but technical writing is already so dense and hard to understand that introducing any ambiguity makes it infinitely more muddled.  It's not hard to give "this" an argument.

-Avoid the verb "to use" at all costs.  That means never say "use," "used, "using," etc...  The verb "to use" is one of the worst words in the english language because it can substitute for almost any other verb and completely destroys the structure of every sentence.  In that sense, "use" is the evil verb cousin of the evil noun "we."  They are both crutches to avoid thinking about sentence structure.  The verb of a sentence should express the main action- that's what a verb is for.  And you can be sure it is almost never "to use."  When your brain wants to say "use," think about what the real verb is that actually expresses the action you are talking about.  Often, if you think of it, it will cause the sentence to be totally reorganized- as it should be.

I'd grep through your writing for "we" and "use" just to see how nasty and pervasive these words can be.  Once you see it, it's like a revelation.  They are a pollutant that every untrained technical writer I have ever seen has to fight to extinguish.

By the way, in personal correspondence and other kinds of writing, these rules are unimportant.  You'll probably find "use" and "we" all over this website and in many of my posts on the NEAT Users Group.  It's taxing to write without them- but that's how it should be.  Professional writing (i.e. for publication) is supposed to take effort.

Some more tips:
-Emphasis is bad: "The results were good" usually sounds better than "The results were very good."
-No fancy words: Use the simplest vocabulary possible to make the point.
-Words must add information;  if they don't, cut them.
-Don't advertise: Impressive results are more impressive if you don't display a need to advertise them.
-Objective language: Science is not subjective.
-Don't use actually or actual: "The ANN actually solved the problem."  "Actually" does not add information.
-Don't use "seems that" or "essentially": These expressions are wordy and conversational.
-No colloquialisms
-Be careful about the difference between "that" and "which."  "Which" is for parenthetical phrases.
-Semicolons: Learn to use them; they are useful.
-e.g. and i.e.: Often more convenient than writing "for example" or "in other words."
-Reduce expresseions with "of."  "The results of the experiment" could be "The experimental results."
-Use descriptive verbs: Verbs are the best way to explain what's going on.
-Reduce the use of "in which": It isn't usually necessary.
-Use proper technical language: If you know a concept is well known (even if you don't know the word for it), don't invent new terminology.  Find out the words people usually use and stick with them.
