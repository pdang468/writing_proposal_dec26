# Lynn Hunt, "How Writing Leads to Thinking" (Perspectives on History, February 2010)

The thesis: writing is not the transcription of finished thoughts. Writing produces the thoughts. Clarity comes after drafting, not before it.

Rules Hunt gives, as process rules for a drafter.

1. **Do not reread notes to start.** Rereading and reorganizing notes feels like work and produces no page. Only prose arguments move the thinking forward; notes keep it "stalled in the fog of infinite possibilities".
2. **The radish rule.** The page count rises every day, three steps forward and two back: write two or three pages, revise them the next day while adding two or three more. Tearing up every day's pages, changing tack daily, or waiting for inspiration is a psychological problem, not an intellectual one.
3. **Momentum needs tunnel vision.** Another document could always be consulted. Limit the scope to what the time allows; second-guess just enough to make revision productive.
4. **Revision is the main work.** "Weeding, thinning, mulching, and watering": the draft exists so that it can be cut. Another reader's eye is sunlight.
5. **Opacity is fear.** Jargon, circuitousness, passive voice and reluctance to get to the point hide the writer from criticism; the hiding costs the reader's comprehension and earns nothing. Say forthrightly what you think.
6. **Authenticity.** Most mistakes come from not saying what one thinks, or from not yet knowing it. Writing is how one finds out.
7. **Read as a writer.** When a text grabs you, ask how the author did it, which quality you cherish, and what you can take from it.
8. **Writing is learned, for life.** Imitation, correction, and attention; no one is born a writer.

How these fit the sentence rules. Hunt's rules govern the drafting process; Ken's and Gopen and Swan's govern the sentences of the revised draft. Applying sentence rules while drafting the first page breaks the radish rule. Order of work: draft to find the argument (Hunt); then revise structure so each sentence puts old information first and the new point last (Gopen and Swan); then strip "we", "use", bare "this", and emphasis (Ken). Hunt's rule 5 and Ken's "no we" pull in opposite directions on the passive voice: Hunt lists it among the hiding places, Ken accepts it over "we". Gopen and Swan resolve the tension: the passive is fine when it puts the right thing in the topic position, and a hiding place when it removes the agent the reader needs.
