# Gopen and Swan, "The Science of Scientific Writing" (American Scientist, 1990)

The thesis: readers interpret prose by where information sits in a sentence, not only by what the words say. A writer who puts each kind of information where the reader expects it frees the reader's attention for the substance. The seven principles, in the authors' order, each with the move it asks for and the failure it cures.

| # | Principle | The move | Cures |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Follow a grammatical subject as soon as possible with its verb | Keep subject and verb adjacent; move interrupting material before or after the clause | Reader holds the subject in memory while wading through a qualification; the interruption is read as unimportant |
| 2 | Put the new information the reader should emphasize in the stress position | End the sentence (or the clause before a colon or semicolon) on the one new thing | The important point hides mid-sentence; the sentence ends on an afterthought |
| 3 | Put the person or thing whose story the sentence tells at the beginning, in the topic position | Open with the agent of the sentence's action | The reader cannot tell whose story is being told |
| 4 | Put old information in the topic position, for linkage backward and context forward | Open with a term the previous sentence left the reader holding | Each sentence starts cold; paragraphs read as lists of facts |
| 5 | Articulate the action of every clause in its verb | The verb names what happens; nominalizations ("the inhibition of") become verbs ("inhibits") | Weak verbs ("is", "occurs", "use") carry no action; the action hides in a noun |
| 6 | Provide context before asking the reader to consider anything new | Old before new, at every scale: sentence, paragraph, section | The reader meets the new thing before knowing why it matters |
| 7 | Make the emphasis the structure signals match the emphasis the substance deserves | Check that stress positions hold the claims and that minor material sits in the middle | Structure advertises the wrong point |

Two corollaries from the article's examples.

- One unit of discourse, one point. A sentence that makes two points, or a paragraph that makes two, forces the reader to choose which the writer meant.
- The stress position can be multiplied by a colon or semicolon, each of which creates a new stress position inside the sentence.

The authors' own caveat: none of these are rules. Every expectation can be violated to good effect, but only by a writer who fulfills it most of the time, so that the violation reads as deliberate. A personal style is the sum of a writer's habitual structural choices; because the habits are consistent, they can be changed.

How the principles fit the advisor's rules: Ken's "never we" removes the author from the topic position so that the thing under study can take it (principle 3). Ken's "never use" and "the verb names the action" are principle 5. Ken's "no bare this" protects the backward link of principle 4. Ken's "no emphasis words" is principle 7 done by structure instead of by adverbs. The two sets do not conflict; Gopen and Swan supply the mechanism behind Ken's prohibitions.
