\section{Second-Round Rewriting}\label{sec:attack:rewriting}

The three decoders yield reconstructions of differing quality (Section~\ref{sec:attack:main}), and a second round tests whether combining them improves on the best decoder.
A language model receives the three reconstructions and rewrites them into one text.
The experiment is exploratory.

\paragraph{Protocol.} The language model is Claude Haiku~\vf{5.5}.
It receives the three reconstructions of the hybrid fit at the \vf{90}-row leak, with the SmolLM reconstruction first, and returns one text.
Its instruction reads ``You get three noisy copies of the same question. Copy 1 is the most reliable; start from it and apply majority rules where the copies disagree. Fix spelling, broken words and repeats. Reply with the text only, inside \texttt{<text></text>}.''
On the two legal datasets the instruction says ``SEC filing excerpt'' in place of ``question''.
The language model receives no hidden state and no part of the true prompt.
The results are reported on \vf{450} selection prompts that played no part in choosing the instruction.

\paragraph{Score.} The language model can change capital letters and punctuation.
Word-F1 in this section is therefore computed on lower-cased words without edge punctuation, for the decoders and for the rewrites alike.
Under the word-F1 of the other sections, the added marks alone lower the rewrite on NQ by \vf{0.12}.

\paragraph{Result.} The baseline is the best single decoder, SmolLM, since an adversary would keep the best reconstruction it holds.
Rewriting the three reconstructions exceeds SmolLM alone on the two legal datasets and matches it on NQ (Figure~\ref{fig:attack:rewriting}).
On legal100t, word-F1 rises from \vf{0.429} to \vf{0.546}, an improvement of \vf{0.117} with a \vf{95} percent interval of \vf{0.101} to \vf{0.133} from a paired bootstrap over prompts.
On legal10w, word-F1 rises from \vf{0.684} to \vf{0.745}, an improvement of \vf{0.061} with an interval of \vf{0.048} to \vf{0.074}.
On NQ, SmolLM alone already scores \vf{0.892}, and the improvement is \vf{0.008} with an interval of \vf{0.001} to \vf{0.017}.
The improvement is therefore largest where the best decoder is weakest.

Two comparisons locate the improvement.
Rewriting the SmolLM reconstruction alone changes word-F1 by at most \vf{0.033}, so most of the improvement arrives with the two further reconstructions.
Under the mismatched-prompt control, rewriting adds at most \vf{0.021}, so the improvement depends on the prompt's own reconstructions.

\paragraph{Examples.} Two legal10w prompts show what the second round does (Table~\ref{tab:attack:rewriting-examples}).
In the first, each decoder loses different words, and the rewrite keeps the words on which two decoders agree.
Word-F1 rises from \vf{0.57} for SmolLM alone to \vf{0.90}.
In the second, the rewrite takes the first word from Qwen, and it keeps the wrong day \emph{19}, on which two decoders agree, with the wrong year of SmolLM.
Agreement between decoders therefore corrects an error of one decoder and preserves an error that two share.
Over the \vf{450} prompts, rewriting raises word-F1 on \vf{51} percent, leaves \vf{34} percent unchanged, and lowers \vf{14} percent.
